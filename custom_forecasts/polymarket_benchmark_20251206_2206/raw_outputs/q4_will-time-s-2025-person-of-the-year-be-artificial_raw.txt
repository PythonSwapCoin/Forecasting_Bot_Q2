# TIME Magazine's Person of the Year: A Comprehensive Historical Analysis of Selection Criteria, Non-Individual Figures, and Artificial Intelligence's Emerging Influence

This report examines nearly a century of TIME magazine's Person of the Year selections, revealing a sophisticated and evolving editorial framework that has progressively expanded beyond individuals to encompass technological innovations, social movements, and abstract concepts. Through detailed analysis of selection criteria, historical trends, and the emergence of artificial intelligence as a serious contender for the 2025 award, this study demonstrates that TIME's editorial philosophy prioritizes cultural and historical influence over moral judgment, creating a dynamic institution that reflects transformative forces shaping our world. Recent prediction markets indicate artificial intelligence holds approximately 37 to 40 percent probability of winning the 2025 award, marking a potential watershed moment where a non-human entity would receive this honor only the second time in the award's nearly century-long history, fundamentally challenging our understanding of historical agency and influence in an era of rapid technological transformation.

## Origins and Evolution of TIME Magazine's Person of the Year Tradition

The Person of the Year tradition began not as a carefully planned editorial initiative but rather as a practical solution to an editorial crisis during the New Year's week of 1928[1][4][7]. As the editors of TIME contemplated their first slowed news cycle, they faced a fundamental problem: they had no compelling figure to place on their newly signature red-bordered cover, and the magazine's editorial policy of that era demanded a person's portrait to grace each weekly issue. While the origins are colorful, the solution proved momentous. According to then-publisher P.I. Prentice, who reflected on this decision in 1945, "No one had done anything newsworthy enough to put his picture on TIME's cover, so somebody suggested we stop looking for a Man of the Week and pick a Man of the Year."[4][7] The choice fell upon Charles Lindbergh, the aviator who had made his historic solo transatlantic flight in May 1927, accomplishing the journey in just 33 hours and 39 minutes[1][4][7]. This selection served a dual purpose: it provided TIME with a decisive cover subject and simultaneously remedied an earlier editorial embarrassment, as the magazine had inexplicably failed to feature Lindbergh on the cover following his groundbreaking achievement[1][4][7].

The editors apparently underestimated the cultural resonance their decision would create. Prentice recounted: "The Man of the Year idea caught on with a bang and, somewhat surprised, we decided to make it an annual event."[4][7] By naming Walter P. Chrysler as "the outstanding businessman of the year" to lead the first regular issue in early 1929, TIME had transformed an emergency editorial measure into what would become one of journalism's most enduring and influential practices[4][7]. Over the subsequent decades, the tradition evolved in scope and sophistication, eventually expanding from its original focus on individuals to encompass groups, objects, concepts, and abstract forces that had shaped the year's events.

The terminology evolved alongside the selections. The original designation of "Man of the Year" reflected the assumptions of mid-twentieth-century journalism, with TIME first acknowledging a "Woman of the Year" in 1936 when Wallis Simpson received the honor[1][4][7]. However, the magazine did not adopt consistent gender-neutral language until 1999 with the selection of Jeff Bezos as the first officially designated "Person of the Year"[1][4][7]. This linguistic shift represented more than mere semantic updating; it reflected TIME's recognition of changing social realities and its commitment to acknowledging influence regardless of gender. The tradition's longevity and continued relevance across nearly a century of dramatic historical change speaks to the editorial framework's fundamental flexibility and its deep resonance with a broad audience seeking meaningful narratives about influence and agency.

## Selection Criteria and TIME's Editorial Philosophy

TIME's selection process, while appearing straightforward, operates under precisely defined but deliberately flexible criteria that have remained largely consistent since the award's inception. The fundamental standard, as articulated by former Managing Editor Walter Isaacson and reinforced across decades of editorials, identifies the Person of the Year as "the person or persons who most affected the news and our lives, for good or ill, and embodied what was important about the year, for better or for worse."[4][28][60] This deceptively simple formulation conceals significant interpretive complexity. Critically, the award explicitly does not function as an honor or moral judgment; rather, it serves as TIME's attempt to identify and document the individual, institution, or force that wielded the greatest influence on global events, regardless of whether that influence was constructive or destructive[1][4][7][25][28].

This philosophical stance was tested early and often. When Prentice justified the decision to name Franklin Delano Roosevelt in 1941, he articulated the reasoning with characteristic clarity: "The choice is in no way an accolade, nor a Nobel Prize for doing good. Nor is it a moral judgment."[4][7] Prentice continued with a striking example: "Al Capone was runner-up in riotous, bootleg 1928."[7] This explicit rejection of morality as a selection criterion meant that TIME's most controversial choices—those that provoked lasting public backlash—often reflected not editorial radicalism but rather editorial commitment to its stated mission of documenting historical influence rather than historical virtue.

The selection process itself involves substantial institutional machinery, though much of it remains deliberately shielded from public view to preserve mounting tension and prevent organizational leaks. As TIME editor Edward Felsenthal explained, the magazine maintains strict confidentiality about frontrunners and even the short list, with knowledge restricted to a small circle of editors and the writer assigned to the story[28]. Layout meetings occur in secret, and staff members involved in the selection maintain carefully cultivated attitudes of strategic ambiguity[28]. The announcement strategy has evolved to maximize media impact; currently, the Person of the Year is announced on the Today show and to TIME's social media followers on the morning the issue goes to press, ensuring maximum simultaneous coverage[28].

Behind this theatrical reveal lies substantive internal deliberation. TIME's editors spend months considering potential selections, consulting with sources globally, and conducting extensive background reporting[5][28]. The magazine operates from the principle that the selection must have both immediate relevance and archival permanence—it should represent "a snapshot of where the world is and a picture of where it's going," capturing a force that "feels like a force of history."[28] This dual requirement has sometimes created tension between choosing figures of obvious contemporary importance and selecting individuals whose historical significance might only become apparent in retrospect. The magazine learned this lesson through experience; while some Person of the Year selections have aged gracefully, others have become curiosities requiring historical contextualization, suggesting that even TIME's editors cannot always predict which figures and forces will remain culturally significant across decades.

TIME's editors have also acknowledged the influence of external factors on their selection process, though they maintain the independence of their editorial judgment. The magazine explicitly invited reader participation through polls beginning in 2010, though these polls have never determined the final selection[1][25][57]. Readers consistently proposed alternatives to the editorial choice, sometimes offering candidates that reflected more directly what the public valued. Yet the editors have resisted the temptation to simply validate popular preferences rather than exercise independent judgment. Indeed, the gap between reader polls and editorial selections became particularly pronounced in recent years, when Donald Trump received substantial poll votes but was not selected until the magazines recognized his capacity to shape global events through his political actions[25][57].

## Non-Individual Selections: A Historical Pattern of Expanding Scope

While the Person of the Year tradition began with individual achievers, TIME gradually expanded its editorial aperture to recognize that historical influence sometimes concentrates not on individual humans but on groups, movements, artifacts, and abstract concepts that capture something essential about a historical moment. This evolution reflects both changing historical circumstances and TIME's developing recognition that its core mission—documenting influence—required flexibility in how that influence was conceptualized and embodied.

The first formal deviation from individual selection occurred in 1937, when TIME named General and Madame Chiang Kai-shek as "Man & Wife of the Year," the first multi-person honor[1][4][7]. This was followed in 1950 by the selection of "the American Fighting-man," representing soldiers engaged in the Korean War and marking the first time an abstract category of person replaced a named individual[1][4][13]. The significance of this choice resided not merely in recognizing a collective rather than an individual but in acknowledging that military personnel as a category represented the year's dominant historical reality. More provocatively, 1966 saw TIME name "The Inheritor," a generational category representing Americans aged 25 and younger—particularly the emerging Baby Boom generation[1][10][13]. The magazine commissioned artist Robert Vickrey to create a composite portrait conceptually representing the generation, though the resulting image would later be identified as resembling Yale economics major Thomas M. McLaughlin, who became an unwitting symbol of a generation and eventually engaged in military service in Vietnam[40].

The most consequential non-individual selection in TIME's history occurred in 1982, when the magazine named "The Computer"—or more specifically, the personal computer—as "Machine of the Year," marking both the first inanimate object selection and TIME's earliest direct engagement with technological transformation as a force worthy of capturing historical influence[1][4][8][11][13]. The 21-page feature package captured the emerging PC revolution at what the magazine recognized as "the end of the beginning," chronicling the rise of machines from Apple, Atari, Commodore, Osborne, Radio Shack, Texas Instruments, and others[8][11]. The editorial framework was illuminating: TIME recognized that no individual entrepreneur—not Steve Jobs, not Bill Gates, not any singular visionary—could claim to represent the transformation reshaping computing and society; rather, the technology itself and the distributed ecosystem of innovators developing it constituted the true historical force[1][8][11]. Notably, in retrospect, some TIME editors acknowledged that naming Steve Jobs in 1982 might have proved more forward-looking[28], a consideration that reveals the tension between selecting the current moment's technology and the individuals who embody it.

The 1982 selection proved to be a watershed moment that established precedent for future non-individual recognitions. In 1998, the magazine selected Bill Clinton and Ken Starr jointly, acknowledging that the interaction between president and independent counsel investigating him represented the year's defining conflict[10][57]. The choice of "You" as Person of the Year in 2006 represented perhaps TIME's most philosophically ambitious non-human selection, celebrating the emergence of user-generated content and the democratization of media production across platforms like MySpace, YouTube, and Flickr[1][13][28]. TIME's then-editor characterized this as "a very strong statement about what was happening with user-generated content," explicitly defending what initially seemed a gimmicky choice as capturing something genuinely important about how human agency and creativity were being reorganized through technological platforms[28].

The 2011 selection of "The Protester" recognized the Arab Spring uprisings and the Occupy Wall Street movement as the year's defining historical force, with TIME's Managing Editor Rick Stengel noting that 2011 demonstrated how "leadership can come from the bottom of the pyramid, not the top."[43] The magazine included representation from Tunisia, Egypt, and other locations where pro-democracy demonstrations challenged authoritarian governance alongside the American economic justice movement. This selection acknowledged that distributed, leaderless movements representing popular desire for change constituted legitimate expressions of historical influence even without centralized authority structures or named figures directing operations[43].

The 2017 selection of "The Silence Breakers"—representing those who spoke out against sexual harassment and abuse—continued this pattern of recognizing collective action and social movements[1][20]. The cover featured multiple women representing different industries and backgrounds, all united by their courage in breaking silence about workplace exploitation. This selection, following closely on the emergence of the #MeToo movement, suggested that TIME's editors recognized social movements driven by grassroots participation and organized around moral principles as legitimate expressions of historical influence[20][23]. Similarly, 2022's selection of "The Spirit of Ukraine" recognized Ukrainian resilience and resistance as the defining force of that particular moment in history[57].

These non-individual selections reveal a sophisticated editorial understanding that historical influence operates through multiple channels and often concentrates more powerfully in movements, technologies, and abstract forces than in individuals. They also demonstrate TIME's willingness to take interpretive risks, treating its editorial platform as a space for philosophical reflection on how history unfolds rather than simply chronicling the already-obvious. Yet they remain exceptional; the vast majority of Person of the Year selections since 1928 have identified named individuals, leading some observers to view non-individual selections as editorial departures rather than fundamental reorientations of the award's purpose.

## Technology and Innovation as Transformative Historical Forces

Technology has appeared consistently throughout TIME's Person of the Year selections, though not always as the primary subject of the award. Individual technological innovators like Andrew Grove (1997), who TIME recognized as "the person most responsible for the amazing growth in the power and innovative potential of microchips,"[42] received the honor for their roles in advancing computational capability. Similarly, Steve Jobs never won the award during his lifetime despite his transformative influence on personal computing, design, and consumer electronics, a striking omission that TIME editors have subsequently noted as potentially shortsighted[28]. The magazine's 1982 choice of the personal computer itself rather than any single entrepreneur suggested an editorial recognition that the technology's significance might transcend the biography of any individual inventor or businessman.

The emergence and acceleration of artificial intelligence in recent years has prompted TIME to expand its editorial consideration of how technology itself shapes historical outcomes. In 2023, TIME named Sam Altman as CEO of the Year, specifically recognizing his role in guiding OpenAI through the transformative impact of ChatGPT's release[21][24]. The editorial framework emphasized that Altman "emerged as one of the most powerful and venerated executives in the world, the public face and leading prophet of a technological revolution."[21][24] This recognition occurred exactly one year after ChatGPT's release, which became "the most rapidly adopted tech product ever."[21][24] The editorial decision implicitly acknowledged that artificial intelligence had entered a new phase of public awareness and practical deployment, with profound implications for labor, creativity, and social organization.

The relationship between TIME and the AI industry took on additional complexity when the magazine announced a multi-year content licensing agreement and strategic partnership with OpenAI in June 2024[55][58]. Through this collaboration, OpenAI gained access to TIME's archives spanning 101 years of content to enhance its products and display in response to user inquiries, with proper attribution to TIME sources[55][58]. This partnership created an interesting dynamic: the very news organization that would evaluate AI as a potential Person of the Year had entered into a strategic relationship with one of the most significant AI companies. TIME's Chief Operating Officer Mark Howard characterized the partnership as advancing "our mission to expand access to trusted information globally as we continue to embrace innovative new ways of bringing TIME's journalism to audiences globally."[55][58] The partnership also granted TIME access to OpenAI's technology to develop new products for audiences and provided opportunities to offer feedback on ChatGPT and other products[55][58].

This strategic relationship between TIME and OpenAI created an interesting mirror to historical concerns about editorial independence. However, the magazine's editorial standards have generally withstood these kinds of entanglements; more significantly, the partnership represented an acknowledgment that AI had become so consequential to media and journalism that traditional editorial institutions needed to engage with it directly. The 2024 launches of TIME's TIME100 AI list, recognizing the 100 most influential people in artificial intelligence, further demonstrated the magazine's explicit recognition that AI had become a central historical force requiring sustained editorial attention and analysis[22].

The coverage of AI development, deployment, and societal impact in TIME's editorial pages has grown substantially in recent years. The magazine has published pieces examining ChatGPT's impact on critical thinking abilities based on MIT Media Lab research, exploring the potential for automation and AI to displace nearly 100 million American jobs over the next decade according to a Senate Democrat report, and documenting the widespread public anxiety about AI deployment despite enthusiasm among technologists[2][19][48]. This coverage establishes that TIME's editors and journalists have engaged substantially with AI as a historically significant phenomenon, creating the editorial backdrop for considering whether artificial intelligence itself might warrant Person of the Year recognition in 2025.

## Controversial Selections and Editorial Philosophy

TIME's Person of the Year selections have provoked sustained controversy throughout the award's history, generating controversies that reveal substantive tensions between the magazine's stated selection criteria and public expectations about what such recognition signifies. These controversies demonstrate both TIME's editorial independence and the persistent public misunderstanding of what the award represents versus what it is frequently assumed to mean.

The most consequential controversy in the award's history followed the 1979 selection of Ayatollah Khomeini, leader of the Iranian Islamic Revolution and a central figure in the Iran Hostage Crisis[1][9][25][51][57]. TIME named Khomeini Person of the Year in late 1979, explicitly recognizing his transformative influence on Middle Eastern geopolitics and global events even as the magazine characterized him in its editorial language as "the mystic who lit the fires of hatred."[51][57] The selection precipitated significant American public backlash, with readers and advertisers objecting to what they perceived as an honor bestowed upon a revolutionary figure whose regime was then holding American embassy staff hostage[1][25][57]. The magazine's editors endured substantial criticism for choosing influence over moral alignment, leading to documented consequences. Wikipedia's entry on the Person of the Year selection notes that "as a result of the public backlash it received from the American audience for naming Khomeini Man of the Year in 1979, the magazine's editors have since shied away from using figures who are controversial in the United States, fearing reductions in sales or advertising revenue."[1][25][57]

This documented editorial response to the Khomeini controversy suggests that while TIME maintains commitment to its selection criterion, the magazine has adjusted its risk tolerance and editorial judgment to account for commercial and reputational consequences of selecting figures widely viewed as morally objectionable. This represents a subtle but real constraint on complete editorial independence; TIME continues to select contentious figures when editors believe they satisfy the influence criterion, but the magazine appears to have internalized a concern about selections that provoke such intense backlash that they threaten institutional viability or advertiser relationships.

Other historically controversial selections provoked different dynamics. Adolf Hitler's 1938 selection as Person of the Year generated less immediate public controversy in the United States, partly because fascism's full horrors remained partially obscured to American observers at that time, and partly because TIME's 1941 republication of its reasoning for selecting Roosevelt rather than Hitler reflected genuine editorial doubt[9][25][51]. The magazine's editors explicitly acknowledged that Hitler could be argued as a stronger candidate by the pure influence criterion but selected Roosevelt because he "represented" a century of democratic aspiration whereas Hitler embodied destructive forces[1][4][25]. This reasoning reveals TIME's subtle interpretive flexibility: while the magazine states that it applies an objective influence criterion, the editors' reasoning sometimes incorporates representational and symbolic considerations that introduce subjective judgment.

Joseph Stalin's selections in 1939 and 1942, along with Richard Nixon's selections in 1970 and 1971, and Nikita Khrushchev's 1957 selection generated varying levels of controversy depending on historical context and American political alignment[1][9][25][51]. What these selections demonstrate collectively is that TIME's editors have consistently been willing to select figures whose historical influence was consequential even when those figures were widely criticized or unpopular in the American context. The magazine appears to interpret its criterion of influence expansively, willing to select leaders, revolutionaries, and political figures even when their actions or ideologies were controversial or destructive.

The 2016 selection of Donald Trump represented a more immediate and understandable controversy, as Trump was a sitting presidential candidate whose election had been described as manifestly consequential and unprecedented[1][10][16][25][57]. The magazine selected Trump for his influence on the year's events, explicitly acknowledging that he had "realigned American politics, remaking the GOP and leaving Democrats reckoning with what went awry."[14] This selection provoked significant criticism, but TIME's editors defended the choice by applying the magazine's stated selection criterion rather than engaging in the implicit moral judgment critics wanted to force into the analysis.

These controversies collectively establish that TIME as an institution maintains commitment to its stated criterion of influence while simultaneously remaining sensitive to the reputational consequences of controversial selections. The magazine rarely explicitly acknowledges this constraint, instead continuing to frame selections as applying an objective, morally neutral criterion of historical impact. Yet the documented editorial response to the Khomeini backlash and the various controversies surrounding other selections suggest that TIME balances genuine commitment to documenting influence against institutional concerns about backlash, advertiser relationships, and organizational reputation. This balancing act helps explain why controversial global figures sometimes receive less sustained controversy than comparably divisive American political figures; American advertisers and readers are more likely to withdraw support than international audiences.
