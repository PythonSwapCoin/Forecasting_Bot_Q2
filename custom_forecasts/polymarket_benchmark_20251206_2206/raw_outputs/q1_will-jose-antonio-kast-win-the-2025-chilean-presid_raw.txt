# Historical Chilean Presidential Trends, Far-Right Electoral Emergence, and the Political Ascendancy of José Antonio Kast: A Comprehensive Analysis of Voter Behavior Shifts and Party Dynamics

The 2025 Chilean presidential runoff between Communist Party candidate Jeannette Jara and far-right Republican Party leader José Antonio Kast represents the culmination of decades-long transformations in Chilean electoral politics that have fundamentally disrupted the traditional center-left and center-right coalition system that governed the nation since its transition to democracy in 1990[1][2]. With Jara securing 26.85% of the first-round vote and Kast winning 23.92%, a stark ideological polarization has become visible in what was once considered Latin America's most stable democracy[2][4]. The combined support for right-wing candidates reaching just over 50% of the total vote in the first round, while the left achieved its weakest electoral performance since 1958, signals a transformational moment in Chilean political history[4][11]. This comprehensive analysis examines the historical trajectory of Chilean presidential elections, the rise of far-right movements, the electoral evolution of José Antonio Kast from 2017 through 2025, the systematic shifts in voter behavior, and the structural changes in party dynamics that have produced this polarized electoral landscape.

## The Historical Foundation: Chile's Democratic Transition and the Erosion of Political Consensus

The foundation for understanding contemporary Chilean electoral politics must begin with the nation's transition from military dictatorship to democracy in 1990 and the distinctive political arrangements that characterized the subsequent thirty years[57][60]. Following General Augusto Pinochet's seventeen-year military regime from 1973 to 1990, Chile established a bicameral National Congress consisting of a Chamber of Deputies with 155 seats and a Senate with 50 seats, with the president serving a single four-year term, creating institutional structures that temporarily produced unprecedented levels of partisan identification[15][19][20]. In the early 1990s, approximately 80% of Chileans identified with a political party, reflecting the intense partisan alignments created by the conflict between supporters and opponents of the Pinochet dictatorship[57][60]. This initial high partisanship was embodied in two dominant coalitions that fundamentally shaped Chilean politics: the center-left Concertación de Partidos por la Democracia, which united Christian Democratic, Socialist, and other moderate parties, and the center-right Alianza por Chile alliance, which organized right-wing parties around a defense of economic liberalism and market-oriented policies[50][57].

However, this system experienced a dramatic and consistent erosion of partisan identification over the subsequent two decades, with party identification declining from 80% in the early 1990s to less than 20% by 2016, representing one of the most severe partisan collapses ever recorded in a democratic system[50][57]. Multiple scholars have identified the dissolution of the principal political cleavage that had organized Chilean politics—the conflict between dictatorship supporters and democracy advocates—as the fundamental mechanism driving this decline[50][57]. As the immediate historical trauma of the Pinochet regime receded and the country experienced nearly two decades of economic growth and institutional stability during the Concertación governments of Christian Democrat Patricio Aylwin (1990-1994), Socialist Ricardo Lagos (2000-2006), and Socialist Michelle Bachelet's first term (2006-2010), the intensely identity-based partisan bonds that had united citizens around these coalitions gradually dissolved[53][57]. The electoral system itself appeared incapable of channeling emerging demands, particularly after rapid expansion of a precarious middle class created new pressures for social mobility and reform that traditional parties struggled to address[53][59].

In 2012, Chile underwent a significant electoral reform that transformed voting from a compulsory system with automatic registration to one featuring automatic registration combined with voluntary voting, a change designed to increase participation particularly among youth and new voters[20][23]. This reform, supported by center-right parties who believed it would expand the electorate, ultimately had complex effects on electoral participation and representation[23]. Under compulsory voting between 1989 and 2011, registered voters faced potential fines and sanctions for not voting, resulting in consistently lower overall turnout rates than those who might voluntarily participate[20]. When voluntary voting was introduced in 2012 elections, overall participation initially declined but revealed new voting patterns among previously unmobilized electorate groups[20].

## The Constitutional Crisis and the 2019 Social Uprising: Catalysts for Political Restructuring

The political stability that had characterized Chile since 1990 underwent fundamental disruption beginning in October 2019 with the Social Outburst (Estallido Social) sparked by an increase in Santiago metro fares of just 30 pesos[26][29][36]. What began as fare evasion protests by secondary school students rapidly escalated into the largest mass demonstrations Chile had witnessed in decades, with over 1.2 million people marching in Santiago on October 25, 2019, to protest against broader social inequality, inadequate healthcare and education systems, and the privatized pension system[26][29]. The protests revealed multiple intersecting grievances: the education system in Chile exhibited exceptionally high socioeconomic segregation with limited intergenerational mobility, healthcare remained partially privatized with inadequate coverage, and pensions had been consistently underfunded since the 1981 transition to individual retirement accounts[29][32][45]. Between October 2019 and March 2020, the social upheaval resulted in approximately 29 deaths, nearly 2,500 injuries, and 2,840 arrests, with estimated damage to infrastructure exceeding 3.5 billion dollars[26].

In response to the social uprising's demands for constitutional reform, the government under center-right President Sebastián Piñera agreed to initiate a constitutional rewriting process, culminating in an August 2020 plebiscite where 78% of voters approved drafting a new constitution through an elected Constitutional Convention[26][36][34]. The Constitutional Convention election in May 2021 produced remarkable results that signaled fundamental shifts in voter preferences: left-wing forces achieved a narrow majority of seats through the Apruebo Dignidad coalition and independent representatives, while the traditional center-right Chile Vamos coalition secured only 37 seats representing approximately one-third of the 155-member body[8][19][34]. The Convention's draft constitution, prepared by left-leaning dominated institutions and reflecting broad demands for social democratization, was overwhelmingly rejected in a September 2022 plebiscite when 62% of voters opposed the proposed document[19][53].

## José Antonio Kast: Electoral Evolution from Marginal Candidate to Frontrunner

José Antonio Kast Rist emerged as the leader of Chile's new far-right movement, yet his political trajectory began within the traditional right-wing political establishment before his transformation into the face of extremist conservatism[1][12]. Born on January 18, 1966, Kast practiced law and served as a Deputy for the center-right Independent Democratic Union (UDI) between 2002 and 2018, representing districts in the eastern Santiago suburbs of La Reina and Peñalolén[1][12][15]. During his tenure in the Chamber of Deputies, Kast worked within the institutional right-wing framework but distinguished himself through his consistent emphasis on religious and moral issues, earning a reputation for Christian conservative positions but not initially positioning himself as particularly radical within the UDI's political strategy[30]. However, Kast increasingly expressed dissatisfaction with the UDI's willingness to criticize the Pinochet dictatorship, a divergence that eventually prompted his resignation in 2016 when the party refused to adopt his proposed shift toward explicit defense of the former military regime[1][12][30].

In his first presidential campaign in 2017 as an independent candidate, Kast secured 523,375 votes representing 7.93% of the first-round total, placing him in fourth position and barely exceeding the 2-3% support levels that opinion polls had predicted[1][10][15]. His campaign introduced rhetoric and slogans that would ultimately define his political brand, particularly his slogan "make Chile a great country" which observers explicitly compared to Donald Trump's "Make America Great Again" messaging[1]. Despite this marginal initial performance, Kast's campaign revealed the presence of a constituency receptive to far-right populism combined with explicit nostalgia for the Pinochet era, particularly among women voters and those concerned with order and morality[30]. After his 2017 defeat, Kast formally established the Republican Party (Partido Republicano) on March 3, 2018, initially as the movement "Acción Republicana," officially registering with Chile's Electoral Service (Servel) on June 10, 2019[1][12].

The Republican Party's founding represented a deliberate effort to capture disaffected voters within the right who believed traditional parties had abandoned defense of the Pinochet legacy and Christian conservative values[1][12][30]. Kast recruited extensive leadership from the UDI, with more than half of the party's early directive consisting of former UDI members, rapidly establishing regional branches across all Chilean regions by July 2021[1][12]. The party's ideological platform combined explicit retention of Pinochet-era institutional frameworks (particularly the 1980 Constitution) with Christian social conservatism, opposition to abortion and same-sex marriage, restrictive immigration policies, and economically neoliberal positions[12][30]. Kast's personal background reinforced these ideological commitments: as a practicing Catholic with nine children who had been raised in a family with deep ties to Pinochet's regime (his brother Miguel Kast served as the military government's central bank president), he embodied a form of conservative politics that seamlessly integrated religious faith, family values, and explicit nostalgia for authoritarian governance[30][51].

In the 2021 presidential campaign, Kast dramatically improved his electoral position despite operating outside Chile Vamos, the traditional right-wing coalition, whose leadership chose other candidates for the primary process[1][8][19]. Running under the Republican Party banner without participation in coalition primaries, Kast won the first round on November 21, 2021, with 1,964,187 votes representing 27.91% of the total, narrowly leading socialist Gabriel Boric of the Apruebo Dignidad coalition who secured 25.83%[1][8][19]. This result astonished political analysts because it demonstrated that Kast had successfully transformed from a marginal 2017 candidate securing 7.93% into a frontrunner capable of winning the first round against traditional center-right candidates like Sebastián Sichel of the UDI[8][19][30]. However, Kast's 2021 victory in the first round proved decisive but ultimately insufficient, as he suffered defeat in the December 19, 2021 runoff against Boric, losing with 44.13% of the vote to Boric's 55.87%, making Kast the first candidate since 1999 to have led the first round but lost the runoff[1][8][19].

Kast's defeat by Boric in 2021 prompted a significant strategic recalibration of his public positioning, though his core ideological commitments remained consistent[1]. Following the loss to Boric, Kast moderated several of his earlier emphases, shifting toward what observers described as a "more pragmatic profile" while continuing to advocate for core right-wing positions[1]. Notably, he began emphasizing support for reformist former president Patricio Aylwin of the center-left, a rhetorical gesture designed to broaden his appeal beyond the far-right base and cultivate support from social-democratic and center-left sectors disaffected with Boric's radical leftist coalition[1]. This repositioning reflected broader cross-camp alignment between right-wing and center-left actors that had been emerging since the failed 2022 constitutional referendum, where both left and right united to oppose overly transformative constitutional changes[1][3].

## The 2023 Constitutional Council Elections: Far-Right Ascendancy and Party System Restructuring

Following the rejection of the first progressive constitutional draft in September 2022, Chile initiated a second constitutional process through a Constitutional Council elected in May 2023, with significantly different results from the May 2021 Constitutional Convention[1][30][37]. The Republican Party achieved a historic victory, winning 23 of 51 Constitutional Council seats representing 35.4% of votes cast, distributed across both the Republican Party directly and allied candidates from the Christian Social Party[1][30][37]. This overwhelming far-right dominance meant that Kast and the Republican Party would control sufficient representation not merely to influence constitutional discussions but to effectively veto any articles receiving less than a two-thirds majority, providing the far-right with extraordinary institutional power[1][30][37]. The 2023 Constitutional Council elections confirmed Kast's transformation from marginal candidate into commanding force within right-wing politics, effectively surpassing the traditional center-right Chile Vamos coalition that had previously dominated conservative politics for three decades[1][37].

Boric's approval ratings collapsed following the Constitutional Convention's failure and the subsequent second constitutional process rejection, falling to approximately 28-30% by mid-2023 and remaining at those depressed levels through 2025[9][24][37]. The government struggled to implement progressive reform agenda items amid severe legislative fragmentation, with no single coalition holding a clear majority in the 2021-elected Congress[9][37]. Specifically, Boric failed to successfully advance major structural reforms to healthcare, education, and taxation that had constituted central promises of his 2021 campaign, reforms his center-left coalition had demanded during the 2019 protests[9][24]. This gap between campaign promises and governmental performance created political space for Kast to position the Republican Party as offering not radical transformation but rather "stability" and "institutional control" in the face of perceived governmental incompetence[37][59].

In November 2024, the Republican Party formally confirmed Kast as its 2025 presidential candidate, deliberately choosing not to participate in primary elections with Chile Vamos or other center-right coalitions, a strategic decision that maximized Kast's independence while the fractured right competed separately[1]. Kast registered his campaign in August 2025 under the slogan "La fuerza del cambio" ("The Strength of Change"), emphasizing messages of institutional renewal, public order, and economic recovery within a framework of democratic continuity[1]. This campaign messaging represented another tactical moderation designed to make Kast palatable to centrist voters concerned about institutional collapse, even while his underlying policy proposals maintained distinctly authoritarian elements including border walls, maximum-security prisons modeled on El Salvador's brutal facilities, and promises to criminalize irregular migration[1][2][14].

## Voter Behavior Shifts: Economic Concerns, Security Anxiety, and Anti-Incumbent Sentiment

Understanding Kast's electoral rise and the broader shift toward far-right politics in Chile requires careful analysis of the multiple intersecting voter behavior transformations that occurred between 2017 and 2025. Economic stagnation emerged as a fundamental driver of voter dissatisfaction, with Chile's growth rate declining from approximately 2% annually during the Boric administration compared to historical averages exceeding 3% during earlier post-dictatorial decades[21][39][40]. Between 2022 and 2024, Chile achieved only 1.7% annual economic growth despite International Monetary Fund projections suggesting potential for 2.5% expansion, meaning that real wages for many Chileans stagnated while perceptions of economic opportunity deteriorated[21][39]. Unemployment reached 8.5% by 2025, with particular severity among younger Chileans and those seeking formal sector employment, creating resentment toward incumbent administrators blamed for economic mismanagement[39][55].

However, economic stagnation served as only one dimension of voter dissatisfaction driving the rightward shift visible in the 2025 first round results. Security and organized crime emerged as the overwhelmingly dominant voter concern throughout the electoral campaign, with 63% of Chileans reporting regular worry about personal safety according to October 2025 polling by Ipsos, making Chile second only to certain Middle Eastern nations in global anxiety about violent crime[2][7][14]. While homicide rates actually declined between 2021 and 2025 under Boric (contrary to Kast's campaign rhetoric), violent crime had nonetheless increased substantially since 2020, particularly in forms previously uncommon in Chile including organized gang violence, drug trafficking, kidnappings for ransom, and extortion by organized criminal networks like the Venezuelan-based Tren de Aragua[2][7][14][39]. The Boric administration struggled to effectively communicate that homicide rates had declined while acknowledging that qualitatively different forms of organized violence had emerged, creating a perception gap where voters experienced elevated criminality despite actual numerical improvements in traditional homicide statistics[53][59].

Immigration emerged as the second most significant voter concern, particularly in urban areas where foreign-born populations had doubled since 2017, from approximately 5% to an estimated 10% of the total population, with approximately 330,000 undocumented migrants among Chile's 18 million residents[2][7][14]. Many Chileans blamed rising crime on immigration, particularly migration from Venezuela where political instability and economic collapse had generated approximately 800,000 Venezuelan migrants into Chile seeking economic opportunity[21][39][55]. Although research revealed no proven causal correlation between immigration levels and crime rates, the perceptual connection became politically decisive[2][7]. This created conditions where Kast's hardline anti-immigration platform proposing border walls, trenches along the Peru-Bolivia border, forced deportations of undocumented immigrants and their Chilean-born children, and criminalizing irregular entry resonated powerfully with voters experiencing both criminal victimization concerns and cultural anxiety about rapid demographic change[2][7][14][39][55].

The electoral impact of these economic and security concerns manifested most powerfully in geographical variations that revealed sharpening regional polarization previously uncommon in Chilean politics. In the November 2025 first round, Franco Parisi, the anti-establishment independent candidate, dominated Chile's mining-rich northern regions; Jara secured victory in central regions including the capital Santiago region; while Kast prevailed throughout the agrarian southern regions except for the two far-southern Magallanes provinces which favored Jara[4][11][22]. These geographical divisions, combined with widening differences between rural and urban voter preferences, indicated an "increasingly segmented political landscape" that departed from prior patterns where regional differences existed but coalitions maintained cross-regional organization[4][11][22]. Academic analysis revealed that rural regions showed greater electoral divisiveness, with remoter communities voting more distinctly for particular candidates, reflecting differences in socioeconomic composition, religious adherence to traditional values, and exposure to different media ecosystems[32].

Anti-incumbent sentiment proved decisive across the ideological spectrum, with 70% of voters choosing candidates from parties opposed to Boric's continued left-wing governance[2][7]. Yet this anti-incumbent rejection was not merely rightward: independent economist Franco Parisi, running on explicitly anti-establishment rhetoric critical of both left and right institutional politics, secured 19.71% of the first-round vote placing him third, demonstrating that anti-incumbency could channel toward non-traditional alternatives as well as toward the far-right[4][11][37][38]. Most analysts concluded that Parisi's voters, characterized as middle-class professionals skeptical of both traditional coalitions, would likely break toward Kast in the December runoff given their antipathy toward the incumbent government that Jara represented[2][7][11][22].

## Party System Fragmentation and the Collapse of Traditional Coalitions

The transformation of Chilean electoral politics since 2017 involved not merely the rise of far-right candidates like Kast but fundamentally the disintegration of the two-coalition system that had dominated since 1990, replaced by unprecedented fragmentation that saw 17 different parties secure parliamentary representation in 2025 compared to 22 in the fragmented 2021 Congress[4][21]. The center-right Chile Vamos coalition, which had been the dominant right-wing force throughout the post-dictatorial period and unified the UDI, National Renewal, and other conservative parties, experienced a historic collapse of electoral support[1][4][21][31]. In the 2025 congressional elections for the Chamber of Deputies, Chile Vamos secured substantially reduced representation as the "new right" including Kast's Republican Party and Johannes Kaiser's newly formed National Libertarian Party surpassed traditional rightists who had previously dominated conservative politics since 1990[1][21][39].

The Republican Party emerged as the single most-voted party in Chile in the 2025 elections, securing 1,407,614 votes and 31 of the 155 Chamber of Deputies seats, with an additional 5 Senate seats, an unprecedented development for Kast's party that had been constituted only in 2019[1][12][21]. This result indicated not merely growth of far-right support but a fundamental intra-right realignment where the "new right" of libertarians, republicans, and social Christians eclipsed the "traditional right" that had governed throughout the democratic transition[1][21][39]. Importantly, the combined right-wing vote in 2025 exceeded 50% of the first-round total when aggregating Kast, Kaiser, Parisi (classified as populist-right by some analysts), and Evelyn Matthei of the traditional center-right UDI[4][21][37][38].

Simultaneously, the left-wing coalition governing under Boric experienced unprecedented fragmentation and weakness while failing to achieve coalition unity around a single candidate, unlike 2021 when Boric's primary victory unified the left around his candidacy[2][4][11][22][37]. The Communist Party, Socialist Party, and other leftist forces united around Jara as their primary coalition candidate in a somewhat perfunctory manner following a nominal primary process, lacking the ideological cohesion and popular enthusiasm that had characterized Boric's 2021 campaign[2][4][11]. The combined left-wing first-round vote of 26.85% for Jara represented the weakest electoral performance for a left-wing presidential candidate since Chile's transition to democracy in 1990, and substantially weaker than achievements in 1958 when the left had secured greater collective support[4][11][22].

The Christian Democratic Party, which had been the largest center-left force during the immediate post-dictatorial era and retained a claim to moderate politics, formally endorsed Jara in 2025, indicating that the centrist party could not mount an independent center alternative and instead had to subordinate itself to explicitly left-wing governance[1][4][55]. This disappearance of genuinely centrist political offerings reflected broader scholarly observations that the "collapse of the center" resulted not from disappearance of center-inclined voters but from institutional incapacity of centrist parties to articulate coherent moderate governance alternatives[53][59]. The centrist constituencies that had supported Concertación governments and maintained equilibrium between left and right, finding no credible political home in 2025, either abstained or directed their votes toward anti-establishment alternatives including Parisi's populist campaign[4][11][22][53][59].

## The Reintroduction of Compulsory Voting and Electoral Participation Dynamics

One critical institutional change affecting the 2025 electoral results involved the restoration of compulsory voting in December 2022 for the first time since 2012, making the 2025 presidential and congressional elections the first general competitions with mandatory voting in an expanded electorate[20][23][37][40]. This institutional transformation possessed substantial implications because voters required by law to participate under pain of minimal financial penalties differed systematically from voluntary voters, particularly regarding socioeconomic status, education levels, age, and political sophistication[20][23][37]. Research comparing Chilean electoral participation under voluntary versus compulsory voting regimes revealed that citizens voting under compulsory systems included substantially higher proportions of lower-income voters, younger citizens, migrants gaining enfranchisement, and electoral participants lacking previous voting experience[20][23].

Critically, new voters compelled to vote under mandatory voting showed distinct political preferences from regular voters, tending toward religious conservatism on moral issues including opposition to abortion and same-sex marriage, concern with public order and security, and economic pessimism regarding their personal financial situations and national economic prospects[9][23][37][40]. As one scholarly group synthesized following the 2023 Constitutional Council elections which had featured the 2022 mandatory voting restoration, while new mandatory voters "adhere[d] to anti-elitist views," they simultaneously held "positions similar to conservatism (religiosity, public order, attitude to abortion and sexual minorities)," explaining why introduction of mandatory voting in 2022 coincided with Republican Party and far-right ascendancy[9].

The 2025 first-round results demonstrated this dynamic clearly: right-wing candidates collectively surpassed 50% of the total vote while left-wing candidates (excluding the populist Parisi) secured less than 27%[21][37][39][40]. Analysts attributed this divergence partially to mandatory voting's expansion of the electorate to include previously unrepresented constituencies with stronger cultural conservatism and security concerns than habitual voters who had participated under voluntary voting[9][23][37][40]. The reintroduction of compulsory voting in 2022 thus constituted a substantial institutional intervention with direct electoral consequences, shifting voter composition toward populations with different policy priorities and ideological inclinations than prior voluntary-voting participants[9][23][37].

## Economic Inequality, Educational Segregation, and Long-Term Electoral Polarization Drivers

Beyond the immediate conjunctural factors of economic stagnation, security concerns, and immigration-related anxiety, deeper structural factors rooted in Chile's economic organization have produced sustained electoral polarization affecting presidential election outcomes throughout the post-dictatorial period and accelerating in recent elections. Academic research analyzing presidential elections from 1989 through 2021 determined that electoral divisiveness—measured as geographical clustering of different candidates' voters—correlates most significantly with schooling inequality and income inequality at the regional level[3][13][32][45]. The Chilean school system perpetuates exceptionally high socioeconomic segregation with students from poor families concentrated in particular schools lacking resources available to wealthy students' institutions, directly affecting educational outcomes and subsequent wage trajectories[3][13][32][45].

This educational inequality feeds into lifetime income inequality: individuals emerging from segregated lower-resourced schools face limited intergenerational mobility, perpetuating relative poverty and economic precarity across generations despite Chile's middle-income country status[3][13][32][45]. The Gini coefficient measuring income inequality has remained stubbornly elevated throughout Chile's democratic period despite two decades of economic growth, with wealth concentrated among a small elite while large middle-class and lower-class populations experience economic stagnation[3][13][32][45]. This persistent inequality during overall economic growth created what scholars term the "vulnerable middle class," populations that achieved middle-income status through education and employment but remained economically precarious and subject to shocks without stable welfare state protections comparable to developed nations[3][13][53].

Particularly during the administration of center-left President Michelle Bachelet (2014-2018), policy initiatives addressing education, taxation, and labor attempted to confront structural inequality, but simultaneous adoption of multiple reform initiatives created what critics characterized as an "irresponsible flood of simultaneous reforms" generating widespread social anxiety during a period of political and institutional instability[53][59]. These conjunctural tensions exploded into the 2019 Social Outburst, whose underlying causes related not merely to a 30-peso metro fare increase but to accumulated frustration regarding persistent inequality, inadequate public services, and perceived elite capture of Chilean institutions[26][29][36][53][59].

## Synthesizing the Analysis: Structural Transformations in Chilean Electoral Politics

Integrating analysis of Chilean presidential trends from 2017 through 2025, with particular focus on José Antonio Kast's electoral evolution and the broader party system dynamics, reveals several fundamental transformations in how electoral competition functions in Chile. First, the traditional two-coalition system that had organized Chilean politics since 1990 has definitively collapsed, replaced by unprecedented fragmentation and personalistic leadership structures that increasingly supersede institutionalized party organizations[4][21][37][39][53][59].
