# Chile's 2025 Presidential Runoff: Historical Precedents, Polling Trajectories, and Economic Backdrop for a Historic Election

Chile's December 14, 2025 presidential runoff represents a watershed moment in the country's democratic history, pitting Communist Party member Jeannette Jara against far-right Republican Party candidate José Antonio Kast in what observers characterize as the most polarized contest since the return to democracy in 1990[1][2]. The first round of voting on November 16, 2025, produced surprising results that defy conventional expectations for leftist candidates and reveal deep structural shifts in Chilean electoral politics, as Jara secured 26.85 percent of votes while Kast narrowly trailed with 23.92 percent[1][25]. This report examines the historical trajectory of Chilean runoff elections, documents Kast's polling evolution from 2022 through 2025, analyzes the coalition dynamics that will determine the runoff outcome, assesses the positioning of rival candidates whose endorsements prove decisive, and contextualizes the economic conditions that have shaped voter sentiment throughout this tumultuous campaign cycle.

## Historical Context of Chilean Presidential Runoff Elections

### The Development of Chile's Two-Round Electoral System

Chile has accumulated substantial experience with runoff elections since the country's transition to democracy, having conducted multiple presidential contests that required a second round when no candidate achieved the absolute majority needed to win outright in the first voting stage[10]. The two-round system that currently governs Chilean presidential elections was established through the 1980 Constitution and represented a departure from prior practices where Congress selected the president from among leading candidates if no single candidate won an outright majority[7]. This constitutional framework requires that a presidential candidate secure more than fifty percent of valid votes—calculated by excluding spoiled and blank ballots—to claim victory in the first round, and only if this threshold remains unmet does a runoff proceed between the top two finishers[7][25]. Since the reintroduction of democracy in 1990, Chile has consistently employed this system, with the first round constitutionally scheduled for the third Sunday of November in the year before the incumbent's term expires, followed by a potential runoff on the fourth Sunday thereafter[7]. Under current regulations following a 2011 electoral reform, the president-elect takes office on March 11 following their election, a date that has remained constant across every democratic election cycle since 1990[7].

The historical record of Chilean runoff elections demonstrates that the outcome of the first round does not inevitably determine the final result. Between 1978 and 2017 across Latin America more broadly, of the forty-six elections that required a runoff, the first-round winner prevailed in approximately seventy-five percent of cases, yet in twelve instances the second-round outcome flipped entirely, with the first-place finisher losing to the second-place candidate[10]. Academic analysis of these comparative patterns reveals that a "negative consensus" can emerge against the first-round winner when voters perceive that candidate as the greater evil and mobilize to block them, a dynamic that proves particularly pronounced when the first-round margin is narrow and neither candidate commands broad support[10]. The percentage-point difference between the top two finishers matters significantly, though it remains insufficient as a deterministic factor; research shows that flipped outcomes become less likely when the gap exceeds ten to fifteen percentage points, yet even substantial first-round leads have been overcome when effective coalitions materialize to oppose the frontrunner[10]. Importantly, the researchers emphasize that a runoff election represents not a continuation of the first round but rather an entirely new election with its own logic, dynamics, and determinants of voter behavior[10].

### The 1999-2000 Runoff: Setting Modern Precedents

Chile's first runoff election under the 1980 Constitution occurred in December 1999 and January 2000, establishing precedents that would influence subsequent contests[18][21]. Ricardo Lagos of the Concertación coalition, which represented Chile's center-left political establishment, narrowly defeated Joaquín Lavín of the Alliance for Chile in the first round with 47.96 percent to 47.51 percent respectively, a margin of merely 0.45 percentage points that left the outcome in profound uncertainty[18]. The campaign had been marked by significant polarization, with Lavín attempting to capitalize on public discontent with economic stagnation and an unemployment rate approaching eleven percent, while Lagos benefited from a high-profile televised debate forty days before the election where he was declared the clear winner by observers[18]. Despite the extremely narrow first-round margin, Lagos ultimately prevailed in the January 2000 runoff with 51.31 percent of the vote to Lavín's 48.69 percent, a gain of approximately 2.35 percentage points that demonstrated the capacity of the centrist coalition's organizational machinery to consolidate support in the second round[18]. The Communist Party's call for null votes, which had been anticipated to damage Lagos's prospects, ultimately proved insignificant as votes that might have been cast for Communist candidate Gladys Marín transferred to Lagos instead[18]. This contest established the template whereby centrist candidates could achieve electoral victory despite ideological proximity to their opponents by emphasizing organizational capacity, leadership experience, and appeals to institutional stability.

### The 2013 Runoff: Bachelet's Dominant Victory

Chile's 2013 presidential election represented a different dynamic entirely, with center-left candidate Michelle Bachelet securing a commanding first-round victory that translated into even more decisive second-round success[24]. Bachelet, running on a platform emphasizing education reform and responding to demands generated by the 2011–2013 student protest movements, won the first round with 46.67 percent of the vote against center-right candidate Evelyn Matthei's 25.01 percent, a lead exceeding twenty percentage points that signaled her formidable position[24]. In the December 15, 2013 runoff, Bachelet extended her advantage significantly, winning 62.16 percent of the vote to Matthei's 37.83 percent[24]. This contest demonstrated that when a first-round winner commands a substantial margin and represents coalition consensus on major policy issues, the second round typically confirms the first-round result rather than reversing it. Bachelet's coalition victory also reflected broad public support for constitutional reform and education restructuring, priorities that had mobilized voters in her favor despite her party failing to secure the supermajorities in Congress necessary to implement her most ambitious legislative proposals[27].

### The 2021 Runoff: Youth, Change, and Unexpected Victory for the Left

The 2021 Chilean presidential contest provided the most instructive historical precedent for understanding the dynamics that could determine the 2025 runoff outcome, as it demonstrated that determined opposition to an apparently ascendant first-round winner can indeed produce a stunning reversal[12][43]. José Antonio Kast, the same far-right candidate now contesting the 2025 runoff, narrowly won the first round with 27.94 percent of the vote against Gabriel Boric's 25.75 percent, a slim margin of approximately 2.19 percentage points that positioned Kast as the presumed frontrunner[15][43]. Conventional analysis suggested that Kast would consolidate the substantial combined right-wing vote—which exceeded fifty percent when all conservative candidates were tallied—and that voters favoring center-right candidates who failed to reach the runoff would naturally gravitate toward Kast as the conservative alternative[43]. However, Boric successfully executed a centrist repositioning strategy during the three-week period between rounds, emphasizing fiscal responsibility, central bank independence, and moderated economic proposals that attracted centrist voters unsettled by Kast's more extreme social conservatism[52]. Crucially, youth mobilization proved decisive, as the youngest voters activated to oppose what they perceived as Kast's authoritarian tendencies and explicit admiration for the Pinochet dictatorship, while older voters concerned about social stability migrated to Boric as the lesser of two evils[12][43]. Boric ultimately defeated Kast with fifty-five to fifty-six percent of the vote, exceeding most polls that had predicted a tighter contest and demonstrating that anti-incumbent dynamics, effective opposition coalitions, and the ability to frame the choice as between two undesirable extremes can indeed overturn first-round results[12][43]. This precedent remains extraordinarily relevant to 2025 analysis, as Kast now seeks redemption after his 2021 defeat, while Jara attempts to replicate Boric's success in attracting centrist voters alarmed by extreme positions.

## The Collapse of the Boric Administration and Background to the 2025 Election

### Presidential Approval and the Structural Crisis

The trajectory that led to the November 16, 2025 first round and December 14 runoff cannot be understood apart from the catastrophic deterioration of President Gabriel Boric's public standing and the corresponding rightward shift in electoral sentiment across the Chilean electorate. Boric, who assumed the presidency on March 11, 2022, as the youngest president in Chilean history and the victor of a decisive 2021 runoff that seemed to validate the left's electoral dominance, confronted mounting public disapproval as his administration encountered successive defeats on constitutional reform, legislative gridlock, and perceived failures in public security[25][40]. By mid-2023, barely eighteen months into his presidency, Boric's approval ratings had plummeted to twenty-eight percent, with sixty-six percent of Chileans disapproving of his administration according to contemporary polling[25][40]. As the 2025 election approached, approval ratings stabilized at approximately thirty percent, roughly the baseline level at which incumbent presidents typically contest difficult reelection environments, yet this represented a catastrophic collapse from the fifty-six percent of voters who had supported Boric's 2021 runoff victory[3][16]. Analysts attributed this dramatic reversal to three reinforcing crises: economic stagnation that left gross domestic product growth languishing at approximately 1.7 percent annually between 2022 and 2024, legislative gridlock that prevented implementation of the government's most ambitious social programs, and public safety concerns as violent crime emerged as the dominant issue shaping voter decision-making[25][29][38][40].

The constitutional reform project that had animated the left's 2021 campaign proved particularly damaging to government credibility. Following massive social protests in 2019 against neoliberal economic structures, the initial progressive constitutional referendum had attracted substantial support, yet voters decisively rejected the left-influenced constitutional proposals by sixty-two percent in September 2023, followed by even more decisive rejection of the conservative-influenced alternative in a subsequent referendum[25][40]. These back-to-back constitutional defeats signaled that Chilean voters, while dissatisfied with the existing institutional framework, lacked consensus on specific reform directions and blamed the government for squandering political capital on processes that yielded no practical results. By November 2025, these constitutional debacles had become distant memory eclipsed by more immediate economic and security concerns, yet they had fundamentally damaged the left's credibility on institutional reform and created an opening for anti-establishment candidates who could position themselves as alternatives to failed political elites.

### Economic Stagnation and Labor Market Weakness

The economic context confronting Chilean voters in 2025 remained substantially weaker than the pre-pandemic baseline, establishing conditions favorable to anti-incumbent movements. Real gross domestic product growth contracted significantly from pandemic-era rebounds, reaching merely 1.6 percent year-on-year in the third quarter of 2025, a decline from 3.3 percent in the previous quarter that disappointed economists expecting 1.9 percent expansion[36]. The World Bank projected that 2025 GDP growth would reach approximately 2.6 percent, supported by strong exports and consumption recovery, yet this remained below the long-term trend growth rate necessary to generate substantial employment creation or wage improvement[39]. The Scotiabank projection from mid-2025 anticipated average unemployment declining from 10.2 percent in 2024 to 9.7 percent across 2025, yet actual data showed the unemployment rate hovering around 8.5 percent in the July-September quarter, with women experiencing unemployment of 8.8 percent compared to 8.2 percent for men[19][56]. While these figures represented modest improvement from earlier quarters, they remained substantially elevated compared to pre-pandemic baselines and reflected a labor market characterized by weak job creation and high informality, with the informal employment share declining only modestly to 26.2 percent by late 2025[19].

Perhaps more significantly for political sentiment, inflation remained problematic despite progress toward central bank targets. Chile's annual inflation rate stood at 3.4 percent in November 2025, having decelerated from 4.4 percent in September 2025 when utilities, food prices, and healthcare costs surged[30]. The government deficit remained elevated at 2.8 percent of gross domestic product in 2024, projected to decline to 1.5 percent in 2025, and this fiscal situation constrained the government's ability to implement new spending initiatives or provide additional economic stimulus[11][22]. These economic conditions—slow growth, persistent unemployment above eight percent despite formal recovery, and ongoing inflation that eroded purchasing power—created a backdrop in which voters perceived the government as having failed to deliver tangible improvements in living standards. The OECD's comprehensive 2025 economic survey emphasized that while macroeconomic imbalances had largely resolved and inflation had declined, Chile faced persistent challenges including stagnating productivity, growing inequality, and limited social mobility that would require structural reforms beyond the reach of any single presidential administration[9].

### Public Safety and Immigration as Dominant Concerns

The political salience of public safety and immigration cannot be overstated in understanding the 2025 electoral environment, as these issues transcended traditional left-right ideological divisions to dominate campaign discourse and voter priorities. Approximately sixty-three percent of Chilean respondents in an October 2025 Ipsos survey identified crime and violence as their primary concern, the second-highest figure globally and reflecting perceptions of deteriorating security despite complex underlying statistics[3][37]. While Chile's homicide rate actually declined to 2.5 per 100,000 inhabitants in the first half of 2025 from 6.0 per 100,000 in 2024, the rate remained double the 3.0 per 100,000 level from a decade prior, and the perception of insecurity had grown substantially faster than actual crime increases[29][37]. Public discourse emphasized high-profile murders and kidnappings linked to Venezuelan criminal organizations, particularly the Tren de Aragua gang, whose activities shocked a population historically unaccustomed to this specific form of organized violence[29]. Security experts attributed the rise in crime to "significant changes in forms of violence, such as kidnappings, armed assaults, extortion, ambushes on roads and even human trafficking," phenomena that differed qualitatively from historical gang violence and generated acute public anxiety[29].

Immigration emerged as the second dominant campaign issue, inseparable from public safety concerns in voter minds and representing a transformation in Chilean political discourse. Foreign nationals comprised 8.8 percent of Chile's population as of 2024, constituting 1.6 million individuals, double the proportion from seven years prior, with Venezuelans accounting for 42.6 percent of all foreign nationals[29]. The Boric government had actually adopted hardline immigration positions despite its leftist orientation, with President Boric declaring in September 2025 that Chile could not accommodate additional undocumented migrants and responding to labor shortage proposals with emphatic rejection[29]. Yet despite this toughness, the government could not escape responsibility for implementing policies in an environment where the Venezuelan diaspora had swelled dramatically. Specialists noted that the connection between rising immigration and crime increases had been "quickly drawn in public discourse," yet sociologist Carolina Stefoni emphasized that "migration and organized crime must still be analyzed as two entirely separate phenomena" and that "most migrants simply aspire to find work and provide for their families."[29] Nonetheless, these nuanced distinctions proved insufficient to overcome voter sentiment linking immigration to insecurity, establishing conditions favorable to candidates like Kast who positioned restrictive immigration policies as central to their security platform.

## The 2025 First Round: Results and Candidate Positioning

### The Surprising November 16 First Round Outcome

The November 16, 2025 first round produced an outcome that observers unanimously characterized as the most surprising in recent Chilean history, confounding virtually all expectations and setting conditions for an unpredictable December 14 runoff[1][45]. Communist Party member Jeannette Jara, the incumbent left-wing coalition's official candidate, secured first place with exactly 26.85 percent of the vote, yet this plurality represented a disappointing result given her status as the governing coalition's sole candidate and the expectation that the unified left would consolidate behind a single figure[1][25][45]. Jara's first-round percentage notably lagged behind President Gabriel Boric's thirty percent approval rating, suggesting that even voters minimally satisfied with the government opted for alternative candidates, a finding that commentators interpreted as devastating to left-wing prospects[1]. José Antonio Kast of the far-right Republican Party placed second with 23.92 percent of the vote, coming within approximately 400,000 votes of defeating Jara outright and demonstrating the formidable strength of right-wing sentiment despite the conservative vote being divided among multiple candidates[1][14][45].

The most shocking result involved Franco Parisi of the Party of the People, an independent populist economist who had previously contested the 2013 and 2021 elections without success, who achieved third place with 19.71 percent of the vote, substantially exceeding polling expectations that had relegated him to single-digit support[1][13][45]. Parisi's campaign maintained that he represented neither right nor left—neither "facho" nor "comunacho" in Spanish colloquial terminology—and focused on middle-class concerns regarding taxation, government spending, and political reform[8][13][14]. His voters possessed distinctive characteristics according to post-election analysis: they demonstrated strong skepticism toward established political institutions, focused on immediate bread-and-butter concerns, and were disproportionately concentrated in specific geographic regions, particularly the mining-dependent north[1][13][45]. Johannes Kaiser, a libertarian congressman and YouTuber from the National Libertarian Party who represented the disruptive anti-establishment right, finished fourth with 13.94 percent of the vote, demonstrating the capacity of novel candidates operating outside traditional coalition structures to attract voters, particularly young voters and those alienated from establishment politics[1][13][25][45]. Evelyn Matthei, the center-right former labor minister and mayor who had been positioned as a potential frontrunner in mid-2025 polling and represented the traditional right-wing coalition, finished fifth with 12.46 percent, a disappointing result that reflected the collapse of center-right support in favor of more ideologically extreme options[1][3][25][45]. Marco Enríquez-Ominami, the progressive independent candidate, secured 1.20 percent, while Eduardo Artés of the left-wing Patriotic Union achieved 0.66 percent, and Harold Mayne-Nicholls obtained 1.26 percent, collectively representing the marginalization of traditional establishment figures across the political spectrum[25].

The turnout level of approximately eighty-five percent represented a significant mobilization of voters following the reintroduction of mandatory voting in 2022, after a 2012 reform had transitioned Chile to voluntary voting[7][25][44]. First-round voting with mandatory participation mobilized voters whose preferences differed substantially from the voluntary-participation elections of the previous decade, generating what observers termed the "hidden vote" phenomenon whereby actual results diverged from pre-election polling by capturing previously disengaged voters[13][45]. This first-round outcome confirmed several structural phenomena that would shape the runoff contest: right-wing candidates collectively secured approximately seventy percent of first-round votes when all conservative candidates were aggregated, the left's vote share represented its lowest performance since 1958, and new parties and anti-establishment candidates had decisively supplanted traditional coalition structures[1][14][25][40][45].

### Geographic Polarization and Regional Divides

The geographic distribution of first-round votes revealed sharp regional divisions that augured poorly for left-right coalition-building in the runoff. Franco Parisi dominated the mining-dependent north, a region characterized by entrepreneurial sentiment and skepticism of centralized government; Jeannette Jara prevailed in the central regions containing most of the Chilean population and the capital Santiago metropolitan area; while José Antonio Kast dominated the agrarian south, except for the two far-southern regions where Jara retained support[1][45]. These patterns reflected what political scientists characterized as "an increasingly segmented political landscape" in which traditional regional voting blocs had fractured, creating a mosaic of diverse preferences resistant to simple two-candidate consolidation[1][45]. The concentration of Parisi's vote in specific regions rather than distributed evenly suggested that his supporters possessed geographically concentrated demographics and concerns, potentially including small-business owners in the north who feared government regulation and taxation, and made efforts to consolidate his vote behind either runoff candidate more difficult than if his support had been distributed uniformly.

### The Endorsed Candidates and Coalition Alignments

Following confirmation of the first-round results, right-wing endorsement patterns coalesced rapidly behind Kast as the presumed conservative standard-bearer for the runoff. Johannes Kaiser and Evelyn Matthei, the fourth and fifth-place finishers respectively, both immediately endorsed Kast upon his qualification for the runoff[1][13][14][25][45]. Kaiser's endorsement proved particularly significant because it signaled that the libertarian anti-establishment right would consolidate behind the more socially conservative Kast rather than maintaining independence, while Matthei's backing brought institutional legitimacy and the support of traditional center-right voters who had previously comprised the dominant political force[1][14]. The Independent Democratic Union, the National Renewal party, and the Christian Democratic Party all formally endorsed Kast, with the exception of the Christian Democratic Party's surprising decision to support Jara instead of abstaining or backing the center-right candidate, a decision that reflected deep strategic divisions within Chile's traditional center[13][25][40].

Franco Parisi's decision not to endorse either runoff candidate proved extraordinarily consequential for predicting the runoff outcome. The Party of the People formally adopted a position of neutrality, with Parisi's statements suggesting that his voters should independently decide whom to support without organizational guidance[1][2][13][16]. Post-election polling immediately captured Parisi voter preferences, revealing that approximately thirty-seven percent of Parisi's electorate indicated they would support Kast in the runoff, whereas roughly twenty-two percent stated they would vote for Jara, with the remaining forty-one percent remaining undecided[48]. These proportions suggested that Kast would potentially capture the majority of Parisi's voters, though the substantial undecided component introduced genuine uncertainty into predictions. Analysts unanimously identified Parisi's voters as the decisive swing force determining runoff outcomes, a conclusion supported by research on Latin American runoff dynamics indicating that candidates capable of building new coalitions that mobilize non-voters or attract the undecided can overcome first-round disadvantages[10][14][48].

## José Antonio Kast's Polling Evolution, 2022-2025

### Positioning and Party Development (2022-2024)

The trajectory of José Antonio Kast's political fortunes from 2022 through the 2025 first round reflected both the strategic capabilities of his Republican Party organization and the broader rightward shift in Chilean electoral sentiment that created unprecedented opportunities for far-right populism. Following his 2021 runoff defeat to Boric, Kast had languished in relative political marginality throughout 2022, with approval ratings and voting intention data showing him as a peripheral figure in national politics[5][31]. However, the catastrophic performance of the Boric government on constitutional reform—particularly the September 2023 rejection of the left-wing constitutional proposal by sixty-two percent of voters—created openings for conservative politicians to reposition themselves as defenders of institutional stability and critics of excessive reform ambitions[25][31][40]. In May 2023, Kast's Republican Party achieved a dramatic breakthrough by winning the largest share of votes in the Constitutional Council elections, a contest designed to draft a new constitutional proposal, obtaining unprecedented electoral support that positioned the party as the preeminent force on the Chilean right[25][31][40]. This victory transformed Kast from a perennial loser haunting the margins of Chilean politics into the Republican Party's undisputed leader and the presumptive conservative frontrunner for 2025.

The May 2023 performance proved decisive because it coincided with public perception of constitutional failure and government incapacity, establishing a feedback loop whereby conservative electoral success in the Constitutional Council generated momentum that translated into improved performance in subsequent opinion polling[5][31][40]. Kast declared the Constitutional Council results "a mandate for a major change in government," positioning himself explicitly as the antiestablishment conservative alternative capable of delivering the security, economic stability, and institutional continuity that voters increasingly demanded[25][40]. The Republican Party's emphasis on law and order, reduced government spending, strict immigration enforcement, and traditional social conservatism resonated with voters frightened by rising crime, anxious about uncontrolled immigration, and frustrated with economic stagnation[31][40]. Kast's public persona as a practicing Catholic with nine children, founder of his own ultra-conservative party independent of the compromised traditional right, and explicit critic of the Boric administration's perceived softness on crime and borders, proved increasingly attractive to voters seeking authentic alternatives to centrist establishment figures[31][37].

By August 2025, just three months before the election, Kast polled at approximately twenty-five to thirty-two percent across various survey houses, positioning him as one of two plausible frontrunners alongside Jara, with Franco Parisi emerging as a wild-card third possibility[5][20][51].

The consistency of Kast's polling superiority in head-to-head runoff scenarios proved particularly significant for understanding his electoral prospects. Cadem polls conducted in May 2025 showed Kast leading with approximately forty-five percent to Jara's thirty percent in runoff scenarios; June 2025 polling gave Kast fifty percent to Jara's thirty percent; July 2025 showed Kast at forty-seven percent to Jara's thirty-six percent[5][51]. This consistent runoff advantage persisted throughout the pre-election period despite first-round polls showing a more competitive race, suggesting that voters who expressed support for other candidates in first-round scenarios frequently indicated they would support Kast in a runoff against Jara specifically[2][16][20]. ### First-Round Underperformance and Polling Accuracy Issues

The actual first-round results showed that Kast's polling trajectory, while broadly accurate in predicting his emergence as a major contender, systematically overestimated his first-round performance relative to competing candidates. Pre-election polling in September and early November 2025 had generally shown Kast polling in the twenty-seven to thirty-two percent range, positioning him as either equal to or slightly ahead of Jara in first-round voting intention[5][20][51]. The substantial first-round success of Franco Parisi, who exceeded most polling predictions by achieving 19.71 percent versus single-digit polling projections in most models, appeared to account for some portion of the apparent "missing" Kast voters, as final undecided voters split toward alternatives rather than consolidating on the apparent conservative frontrunner[1][45][50].

This polling divergence reflected what analysts termed the "hidden vote" phenomenon characteristic of elections with mandatory voting that capture previously disengaged or politically marginal constituencies whose preferences diverge from voluntary-participation polling samples[1][13][45]. The reintroduction of mandatory voting in 2022 meant that the November 2025 contest represented only the second general election since the adoption of compulsory voting requirements, limiting the availability of validated models for predicting how previously non-voting populations would distribute their ballots across candidates[7][25][44]. Younger voters, newly mobilized voters who had not participated in prior voluntary elections, and voters situated outside urban centers appeared to skew more heavily toward anti-establishment candidates like Parisi than traditional polling methodologies had anticipated[1][13][45]. Kast's organization appeared to have under-mobilized relative to its polling advantage, or alternatively, had over-estimated the consolidation of right-wing voters during a period when the right-wing vote remained substantially fragmented across multiple candidates.

## The Runoff Coalition Dynamics and Endorsement Strategy

### The Evolution of Coalition Backing and Political Alignment

The endorsement patterns following the first round illuminated the coalition dynamics that would determine the December 14 runoff outcome, revealing that Kast had systematically consolidated right-wing support while Jara maintained support primarily among leftist constituencies with limited expansion capacity[1][13][14][25]. Within hours of the first-round results confirmation, all first-round right-wing candidates who failed to reach the runoff promptly endorsed Kast, creating unprecedented unity across the traditionally fractious Chilean right[1][13][14][45]. Johannes Kaiser's endorsement, while predictable given his libertarian positioning at the conservative end of the ideological spectrum, signaled that anti-establishment right voters would consolidate behind Kast despite philosophical differences over economic policy and regulatory approaches[1][13][14]. Kaiser's endorsement proved particularly important because it suggested that younger voters attracted to Kaiser's disruptive candidacy could potentially be persuaded to support Kast despite the latter's more traditional conservative positioning.

Evelyn Matthei's endorsement of Kast represented perhaps the most symbolically significant alignment, as Matthei embodied the traditional right that had dominated Chilean politics from 1973 until the recent rise of anti-establishment conservatives. Matthei, serving as a former government minister, mayor, and parliamentary deputy representing the Independent Democratic Union's center-right liberalism, had been positioned as a plausible frontrunner as recently as mid-2025 before her collapse to fifth-place finish[3][25][45][50]. Her endorsement of Kast signaled the willingness of institutional conservatives to subordinate their preferences to a more ideologically extreme candidate if that candidate possessed superior electoral prospects, a phenomenon political scientists associate with the "lesser evil" dynamic whereby moderate voters accept ideologically uncomfortable outcomes to prevent outcomes they perceive as worse[10][52]. The endorsement also facilitated access to institutional party machinery and campaign resources accumulated by Matthei's organization, augmenting Kast's capacity to mobilize voters in the final month preceding the runoff[1][13][14].

The Christian Democratic Party's decision to endorse Jara rather than abstain or back Kast proved noteworthy given the party's historical positioning as Chile's crucial centrist force mediating between left and right throughout the democratic period[25][40].
