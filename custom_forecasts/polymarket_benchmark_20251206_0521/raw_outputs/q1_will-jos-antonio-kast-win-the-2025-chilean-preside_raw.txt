# The Evolution and Impact of José Antonio Kast's Political Rise: Historical Context of Chilean Presidential Elections and Far-Right Ascendancy

## Summary

This comprehensive analysis examines the trajectory of Chilean presidential elections from the democratic transition through 2025, with particular emphasis on the electoral performance and political significance of José Antonio Kast and the far-right Republican Party. Through three presidential campaigns spanning 2017 to 2025, Kast's vote share evolved from 7.93 percent in 2017 to 27.9 percent in the first round of 2021, before declining marginally to 23.92 percent in the first round of 2025.[1][2][4] The research demonstrates that Kast's rise reflects not primarily an ideological rightward shift among Chilean voters but rather a profound disillusionment with centrist political establishments, compounded by institutional failures including two rejected constitutional reform attempts, mounting public anxiety regarding crime and migration, and the economic disappointment of President Gabriel Boric's administration.[3][4][6] This report provides detailed historical documentation of how credible sources including academic institutions, international media organizations, and electoral authorities have documented these developments, revealing complex voter motivations that extend beyond simple left-right polarization.

## The Historical Context of Chilean Presidential Elections and Democratic Transitions

Chile's contemporary electoral landscape cannot be understood without recognizing the profound historical rupture created by the military dictatorship of Augusto Pinochet, which lasted from 1973 until 1990.[16] The return to democracy in 1990 established an initial political structure centered around two dominant coalitions that would persist for more than three decades: the center-left Concertación and the center-right Alianza (later renamed Chile Vamos).[50] These coalitions, despite their ideological differences, converged toward the political center over time, implementing moderate reforms while maintaining the foundational neoliberal economic model established during the dictatorship.[50] This centrist stability characterized presidential elections through the 2010s, with power alternating between the coalitions in what appeared to be a predictable pendulum pattern.

The 2017 presidential election, held on November 19, represented the last election before the eruption of mass social protests in October 2019, yet it already foreshadowed the emerging instability in Chile's party system.[2] In this election, Sebastián Piñera of the center-right Chile Vamos coalition secured victory with 54.57 percent in the runoff against Alejandro Guillier of the center-left New Majority coalition, who received 45.43 percent.[2] However, the first-round results proved more revealing of underlying discontent. While Piñera led with 36.64 percent, the remaining vote fragmented across multiple candidates, including independent Beatriz Sánchez representing the Broad Front leftist bloc with 20.27 percent, and José Antonio Kast running as an independent with 7.93 percent.[2] This fragmentation signaled the beginning of the traditional coalitions' erosion, though the magnitude of this shift would not become apparent until later elections.

The four years following Piñera's 2017 victory witnessed transformative events that fundamentally altered Chilean political dynamics. Beginning on October 18, 2019, massive social protests erupted across Chile, initially sparked by a proposed increase in subway fares but rapidly expanding to encompass broader grievances regarding economic inequality, inadequate pension systems, and deficient public services.[44][47] The Chilean Social Outburst, as it came to be known, mobilized over one million people and resulted in extensive property damage, forcing the government to declare a state of emergency.[44] These protests created a political opening that ultimately led to the calling of a constitutional referendum in April 2020, which 78 percent of voters approved, with 79 percent choosing for an entirely popularly elected Constitutional Convention to draft a new fundamental law.[15] This extraordinary constitutional moment would dominate Chilean politics for the subsequent three years and shape electoral outcomes in ways that transcended traditional ideological categories.

## José Antonio Kast's Political Trajectory and Electoral Performance

Understanding José Antonio Kast's rise to political prominence requires examining his long career within Chilean conservatism before his emergence as a far-right leader. Born on January 18, 1966, Kast served as a deputy in the Chamber of Deputies for 16 years and maintained membership in the Independent Democratic Union (UDI), one of Chile's traditional right-wing parties, for 20 years.[7][8] The UDI itself carried significant historical baggage, having emerged during the Pinochet dictatorship as a civilian vehicle for authoritarian conservatism, though it moderated its positions substantially after the return to democracy.[36] Kast's dissatisfaction with the UDI's evolution crystallized in 2016 when he resigned from the party in protest, believing that party leadership criticized former dictator Augusto Pinochet too frequently.[7][8] This resignation, prompted by Kast's desire to shift the party toward more emphatic defense of the military regime's legacy, marked a crucial turning point in his political evolution.

Kast's first presidential campaign in 2017 occurred in this context of growing ideological distance from the moderating mainstream right. Running as an independent candidate, Kast finished in fourth place nationally with 523,375 votes representing 7.93 percent of the total votes cast.[2][7] Though his share remained marginal compared to the major coalition candidates, his performance was notable for attracting support from voters who felt alienated by the centrist consensus. During this 2017 campaign, Kast adopted explicitly provocative stances toward Pinochet's legacy, stating "If he were alive, he would vote for me," a declaration that presaged his later explicit alignment with authoritarian nostalgia.[31] His platform emphasized law and order, traditional Catholic values, immigration restrictions, and opposition to abortion and same-sex marriage, creating what scholars would later identify as a novel political formula in contemporary Chilean politics—combining market liberalism with social and cultural authoritarianism.[9]

The 2021 presidential election dramatically elevated Kast's political standing. On November 21, 2021, voters participated in the first round of a presidential election that would fundamentally reshape Chilean electoral competition.[1] Kast's newly established Republican Party, founded in 2019 after his UDI departure, mobilized support around a platform emphasizing security, cultural conservatism, and nationalist appeals.[8] In this election, Kast secured 27.9 percent of first-round votes with 1,961,779 total votes cast, narrowly leading over the leftist Gabriel Boric who received 26.82 percent.[1][7] This result shocked observers who expected the traditional center-right and center-left coalitions to contest the runoff, yet instead Chilean voters had rejected both establishment coalitions in favor of political outsiders. Kast's first-round victory seemingly positioned him for the presidency, yet the runoff election on December 19, 2021, produced a devastating reversal for the far-right candidate.

In the runoff, Boric mobilized significantly greater support, securing 55.87 percent of votes compared to Kast's 44.13 percent.[7] Multiple factors contributed to this second-round outcome. Importantly, both of the other first-round candidates—Yasna Provoste of the Christian Democratic Party and Sebastián Sichel of the center-right coalition—endorsed Boric in the interlude between rounds.[1] Former President Michelle Bachelet also traveled to Chile from her position as United Nations Human Rights High Commissioner to formally endorse Boric.[1] These endorsements signaled that portions of the traditional center and left, while fractured in the first round, could unite against what they perceived as an extremist far-right threat. Additionally, Boric's campaign successfully mobilized youth voters and those motivated by progressive social policy concerns, executing effective mobilization despite Kast's seemingly strong first-round position.[1]

The 2025 presidential election witnessed Kast's third presidential campaign, and the results reflected the complex and volatile Chilean electorate described by contemporary analysts. Held on November 16, 2025, the first round produced another competitive result that necessitated a runoff scheduled for December 14.[19] Kast secured 23.92 percent with 3,092,946 total votes cast, placing him second behind communist party candidate Jeannette Jara who received 26.85 percent with 4,347,151 votes.[4][10][19] The 2025 first-round result represented a decline from Kast's 2021 first-round performance, decreasing by approximately 4 percent in vote share despite an increase in absolute votes due to compulsory voting expansion.[19] Multiple analysts noted that the apparent rightward shift in Chilean politics, commonly noted in commentary on the 2025 results, masked more complex voter dynamics than simple ideological movement rightward.

## The Rise of the Republican Party and Structural Transformations of the Chilean Right

The Republican Party's emergence and rapid ascendancy constitutes one of the most significant developments in contemporary Chilean politics. Founded by Kast in 2018-2019, the party began as what scholars characterized as the Chilean manifestation of a global conservative wave in Latin America, bearing similarities to Spain's Vox party in its formation as a breakaway vehicle for disillusioned conservative voters.[8][31] The party was formally legally constituted through a multi-year process between 2020 and 2021, with regional registrations gradually expanding until achieving national party status by July 2019.[8] This institutional development occurred simultaneously with the constitutional chaos engulfing Chilean politics, as the rejected 2021 constitutional convention draft created openings for candidates offering alternative visions of governance.

The Republican Party achieved extraordinary electoral breakthrough in the May 7, 2023, Constitutional Council elections, a watershed moment that positioned Kast as the dominant force on the Chilean right.[31][34] In that election, the Republican Party secured 3,468,258 votes representing 35.5 percent of the total vote and won 23 of the 50 Constitutional Council seats, establishing itself as the plurality rightist force and surpassing all traditional right-wing parties combined.[31][34] This result represented the highest percentage achieved by any Chilean party since 1965 when Christian Democrats dominated under Eduardo Frei Montalva.[31] Electoral analyst Juan Lindón observed that the party had "bled out" the three traditional right-wing parties—the UDI, National Renewal (RN), and Evolución Política—which collectively achieved only 21 percent compared to the Republican Party's superior performance.[31]

Scholarly analysis of the Republican Party's composition and ideology reveals a distinct configuration compared to traditional Chilean conservatism. According to research from the 2023 Ultra-Lab Electoral Study, support for Kast and the Republican Party exhibits characteristics distinct from support for Evelyn Matthei and the traditional right.[9][21] Kast supporters are characterized by what researchers identify as a configuration combining "moral authoritarianism, anti-feminist attitudes, economic pessimism, and market radicalism," distinguishing them from the more pragmatically oriented supporters of traditional right-wing candidates.[9][21] The ideological doctrine of the Republican Party incorporates what scholars term "organic Pinochetism," representing a revival of authoritarian conservatism rooted in explicit defense of the Pinochet regime's legacy, combined with contemporary right-wing populism.[8][31] This represents a significant departure from the UDI and RN, which had gradually moderated their discourse regarding the dictatorship over the three decades following democracy's restoration.

The party's policy platform combines multiple ideological elements in ways that depart from pure economic conservatism or traditional Catholic social teaching. Regarding economic matters, the Republican Party embraces neoliberal commitments to market mechanisms, tax reduction, and limitation of state activity.[8][32] However, this economic liberalism coexists with emphatic social and cultural conservatism, including opposition to abortion under virtually all circumstances, rejection of same-sex marriage and adoption rights, and organized resistance to what party members characterize as "gender ideology."[8][11][32] Immigration restriction constitutes another core pillar of party doctrine, with the party calling for construction of a ditch along the border with Bolivia and Peru, mass deportation of undocumented immigrants, and criminal penalties for irregular entry.[7][27] These positions appeal to constituencies simultaneously attracted to market liberalism and cultural nationalism, a combination that transcends traditional left-right economic categories.

## Voter Motivations and the Security-Immigration Nexus: Understanding Rightward Shifts

Analysis of voter behavior and preferences in recent Chilean elections reveals that security concerns, immigration anxiety, and economic dissatisfaction have increasingly dominated campaign discourse and voter decision-making, overshadowing the social and constitutional issues that dominated the 2019-2023 period.[3][6][39][40] Polling data from Ipsos conducted in October 2024 indicated that 63 percent of Chilean adults cited crime and violence as their foremost concern, the second-highest figure globally for this indicator.[39] Although Chile maintains one of Latin America's lowest absolute homicide rates at approximately 6.7 per 100,000 inhabitants, the perception of insecurity has profoundly affected public morale, with murders increasing from 1,322 in 2022 to higher levels subsequently.[39][38] This discrepancy between objective crime statistics and public perception reflects what scholars identify as a fundamental dynamic in contemporary democratic politics globally—the power of security anxiety to mobilize voters regardless of empirical crime trends.

Migration constitutes the second axis of voter anxiety driving electoral outcomes in 2025. Chile's foreign-born population has doubled since 2017, reaching approximately 8.8 percent of the nation's 20 million inhabitants by 2024, with an estimated 330,000 undocumented migrants in the country.[39][40] The majority of these migrants originate from Venezuela, fleeing the dictatorship and economic collapse in that nation, with additional populations from Colombia, Haiti, and other Caribbean and South American countries.[38] Right-wing candidates have explicitly associated undocumented immigration with rising criminal activity, despite empirical research indicating no proven correlation between immigration levels and crime rates.[40] This rhetorical linkage has nevertheless resonated powerfully with segments of the Chilean electorate, particularly in regions experiencing rapid demographic change such as the northern border provinces.[4][39][40]

Harvard's David Altman, a professor of political science at the Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile, articulated an influential interpretation of 2025 election results that challenges simplistic ideological readings. According to Altman's analysis presented to the Delegation of the Harvard Deans, "Voters did not suddenly become conservative. They abandoned the center, and the only landing space available was on the right."[6] This formulation shifts analytical focus from voter preference shifts toward right-wing ideology to voter rejection of centrist politics perceived as ineffective and disconnected from ordinary concerns. Universidad Diego Portales scholar Rossana Castiglioni emphasized the role of mandatory voting in introducing new voter cohorts: "Many of these new voters are younger, more economically vulnerable, and overwhelmingly distrustful of institutions. Their frustration fueled surprising outcomes."[6] The reintroduction of compulsory voting for all citizens in 2022 transformed electoral dynamics by bringing previously non-participating populations into the electorate, populations whose voting patterns differed substantially from habitual voters.

The analysis by Patricio Navia, adjunct professor at the Center for Latin American Studies and New York University, identified a cyclical dynamic characterizing Chilean electoral politics since 2009: "As soon as you win, you become the elite that people want to punish next."[6] This observation encapsulates the pattern whereby voters in each successive election reject the incumbent administration irrespective of its ideological positioning, expressing generalized dissatisfaction through electoral punishment. President Boric's administration, despite its progressive positioning, fell victim to this dynamic, with approval ratings declining from robust levels at his March 2022 inauguration to approximately 30 percent by the time of the November 2025 election.[4][10] Boric's government faced criticism regarding its management of crime and migration, as well as its inability to deliver promised economic reforms, particularly given legislative gridlock and the failure of two constitutional reform attempts in 2022 and 2023.[3][15]

## Constitutional Crises and the Delegitimization of Elite Politics

The trajectory of Chilean constitutional politics from 2020 through 2023 fundamentally shaped the electoral landscape confronting voters in 2025. The October 2020 constitutional referendum saw 78 percent of voters approve replacement of the 1980 Pinochet-era constitution, establishing the legitimacy of constitutional reform as a democratic project.[15] Subsequently, the May 2021 Constitutional Convention elections produced a leftist-dominated body that drafted a constitution perceived by significant portions of the electorate as excessively progressive and insufficiently pragmatic. The September 4, 2022, constitutional plebiscite on this first draft resulted in decisive rejection, with 61.87 percent voting "Against" and only 38.13 percent voting "For."[18] This outcome shocked the left and demoralized the constitutional reform project.

Rather than abandoning constitutional reform, President Boric initiated a second attempt through a different institutional mechanism. Elections for a Constitutional Council held on May 7, 2023, produced a dramatically different result, with right-wing parties achieving a three-fifths majority enabling them to freely draft constitutional text without leftist veto.[34] The Republican Party achieved 35.5 percent of the vote and 23 council seats, enabling it to shape constitutional proposals substantially.[34] However, the subsequent December 17, 2023, constitutional plebiscite on this right-wing-dominated council's draft also failed, with 56 percent voting "Against."[15] The consecutive rejection of constitutions drafted by the left and then the right signaled profound disagreement extending beyond elite partisan divisions, suggesting that Chilean voters had become skeptical of comprehensive constitutional transformation.

Analysts and political scientists emphasized that these consecutive constitutional rejections, occurring in September 2022 and December 2023, delegitimized both the leftist government's reform project and the right-wing opposition's constitutional alternative, leaving ordinary voters skeptical of the entire political establishment.[6][15] The Friedrich Naumann Foundation documented that "Hardly anyone could claim victory out of Sunday's results" following the December 2023 plebiscite, with both the government and the Republican Party sustaining political damage.[15] President Boric announced following the second rejection that his administration would not pursue a third constitutional reform attempt, effectively ending the constitutional process that had dominated Chilean politics since October 2019. This declaration of constitutional exhaustion created a political vacuum that contributed to the anti-establishment orientations evident in the November 2025 election.

One of the most surprising developments in the 2025 election involved the unexpected strong performance of Franco Parisi, founder of the anti-establishment Party of the People (Partido de la Gente), who finished third in the first round with 19.71 percent despite residing in the United States and conducting most of his campaign through social media with limited financial resources.[4][10][51] Parisi, characterized as a "social liberal" and populist anti-establishment candidate, accumulated sufficient congressional seats to become a decisive power broker in parliament, despite not advancing to the presidential runoff.[4][10] Academic analysts emphasized that Parisi's outsider status and explicit rejection of establishment politics resonated with voters, particularly younger cohorts and those experiencing economic vulnerability, regardless of his limited organizational infrastructure or traditional political background.[6] The success of such an extraordinary outsider candidate demonstrates the profound delegitimization of established political institutions and traditional political parties.

Professor Loreto Cox of the Universidad Católica de Chile School of Government emphasized that understanding the 2025 election required recognizing the electorate's fundamental transformation: "Voters supported candidates who addressed the concrete preoccupations they've been expressing for some time: the fear, uncertainty, and insecurity that crime, immigration, and the economy present going forward."[10][51] This formulation highlights that voters' decisions reflected pragmatic response to perceived governance failures rather than coherent ideological commitment to far-right or any other ideology. The "politically homeless" characterization employed by Harvard DRCLAS analysts captured the disorientation experienced by voters in an electoral landscape where traditional ideological categories no longer provided reliable guides to political identification.
